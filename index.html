TL;DR

Existing video explanations say that motion matters but not which motion. TraCE clusters optical-flow trajectories into Trajectory concepts and pairs them with Object and Scene concepts, so one typed interface explains both action recognition and VQA, at a cost of about one accuracy point.

Read the abstract

Video understanding models often rely on motion, but faithful explanations must reveal not only whether motion matters, but also which source of motion supports a prediction. We identify motion-source under-specification as a key limitation of existing video XAI: region-centric explanations are source-ambiguous, while concept-based explanations are source-incomplete when their motion vocabulary is restricted to single-person pose sequences. To bridge this gap, we propose TraCE—Trajectory-Aware Concepts for Explainable Video Understanding—a concept-based framework that elevates visual trajectories to first-class explanatory concepts. By clustering optical-flow trajectories, TraCE discovers source-flexible motion concepts that capture dynamic evidence from people, objects, and interactions, while object and scene concepts account for static context. TraCE applies the same typed trajectory/object/scene concept interface to both action recognition and video question answering (VQA), enabling concept-level explanations beyond a single task. We evaluate TraCE across six benchmarks: NTU RGB+D Mutual, Chiral SSv2, UCF-101, KTH, TOMATO, and STAR. Across five random seeds, TraCE provides trajectory-aware explanations of dynamic evidence with a modest 1.0-point average trade-off on action recognition and comparable VQA performance (a nominal 0.4-point average gain) relative to non-interpretable baselines. User studies, faithfulness analyses, temporal sensitivity tests, and concept-intervention results further show that TraCE produces interpretable, faithful, and practically useful explanations.

Start from a black-box video model

A frozen video encoder turns a clip into a feature vector and a classifier turns that into a label. It works, but nothing in between has a meaning you can point at: you cannot ask which motion the decision relied on.

TraCE puts a concept layer between the features and the classifier. Each of its units stands for one concept, and the classifier is only allowed to see those units. The explanation now lives inside the model rather than in a post-hoc heatmap.

The layer is split into three typed groups. Trajectory concepts carry dynamic evidence, Object and Scene concepts carry static context. Keeping them separate is what lets TraCE say which source of evidence a prediction used. Where the trajectory concepts themselves come from is shown in the card below.

A clip flows through, concepts light up, and the sparse linear classifier sums them into a prediction. Because the classifier is linear, each concept's contribution is simply its activation times its weight. The three strongest here are a diving trajectory, the object “soccer player”, and the scene “soccer field”: the same kind of explanation you saw in the viewer above.

Video question answering adds a second input. The question is paired with each candidate answer and encoded by a frozen text encoder, giving one text feature per candidate. The video side is left exactly as it was; only the classifier is set aside.

A text concept layer maps each candidate into the same trajectory, object, and scene concepts, in the same order, as the video side. The result is a relevance vector per candidate: which concepts that answer would need to be true. Trajectory concepts have no names, so this part is learned from answer supervision rather than from text similarity.

For each candidate, the video's activations and the answer's relevance are compared unit by unit. A concept counts only if the video shows it and the answer needs it, so most entries stay silent and a few light up: here a leftward trajectory and the player object for “Left”.

Only the strongest few matches within each concept group are summed, with a learned weight per group, into a compatibility score. The highest score is the answer, and the handful of concepts that produced it is the explanation. That is exactly the PullUps table in the viewer.

Where the trajectory concepts come from

ExtractEstimate dense optical flow on training videos and trace a trajectory from each seed pixel over a short clip around every temporal segment.
FilterKeep foreground trajectories with large motion magnitude; drop weak or background motion.
ClusterPool trajectories over the training set and cluster them (FINCH). Each cluster is one trajectory concept; its medoid is the visual prototype.
Soft-labelCount each video's trajectories per cluster and normalize into a soft presence label that trains the trajectory part of the concept layer. Object and scene concepts come from a Video-LLM with video–text pseudo-labels.

Two studies on motion-focused subsets of NTU RGB+D Mutual and Chiral SSv2. (a) In pairwise comparisons, over 93% of responses prefer TraCE explanations to DANCE and a language-based CBM, and about 78% prefer TraCE to SALF-CBM. (b) Asked how well a concept matches the motion shared by its top-activating clips, participants rate trajectory concepts 4.2 / 5, versus 2.5 for pose-sequence and 2.2 for language-derived motion concepts. In a separate blinded study in the VQA setting (50 participants, 250 ratings per comparison, both models correct on every item), participants prefer TraCE explanations in 70.4% of comparisons against language-derived motion concepts and 85.6% against pose-sequence concepts.
